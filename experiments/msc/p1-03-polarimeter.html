POLARIMETER — SPECIFIC ROTATION OF CANE SUGAR

Aim

To determine the specific rotation of cane sugar solution using a half-shade polarimeter.

Apparatus required

Half-shade polarimeter (Laurent's plate or biquartz), sodium vapour lamp (monochromatic) or white light source, polarimeter tube (2 dm), physical balance, measuring cylinder, beakers, reading lens, cane sugar and distilled water.

Formula

S = θ / (l C)  deg dm−1 (g/cc)−1

where S = specific rotation of cane sugar, θ = rotation of the plane of vibration produced by the solution (degree), l = length of the solution column (decimetre) and C = concentration of cane sugar in the solution (g/cc). From the graph, S = (θ/C) / l.

Principle

Cane sugar solution is optically active (dextro-rotatory). When plane-polarised light passes through it, the plane of vibration is turned through an angle θ proportional to the length of the solution and its concentration (Biot's law, θ = S l C). The half-shade device divides the field of view into two halves whose brightness can be matched very precisely, so the angle through which the analyser must be turned — the rotation θ — can be measured accurately.

Experimental set-up

Theory

The specific rotation of a substance at a given temperature and wavelength is the rotation produced by a 1 dm long column of its solution containing 1 g of the substance per cc: S = θ/(l C).

Light from a monochromatic source S is made parallel by the convex lens L and falls on the polarising Nicol N1, which makes it plane polarised. It then passes through the half-shade device (Laurent's plate), through the tube T containing the active solution and through the analysing Nicol N2, and is viewed with a telescope/eyepiece. A circular scale with vernier attached to the analyser measures its rotation.

Laurent's half-shade plate is a semicircular half-wave plate of quartz (cut with its faces parallel to the optic axis) cemented to a semicircular glass plate that transmits and absorbs the same amount of light. Light through the glass half keeps its vibration OP; in the quartz half the vibration becomes OQ, its mirror image about the optic axis (∠POQ = 2δ). When the analyser is perpendicular to the line of junction, both halves are equally dim. A tiny rotation of the analyser makes one half clearly darker than the other, so this setting is very sensitive. With sugar in the tube, OP and OQ both turn through θ and the analyser must be turned through the same θ to restore equal illumination.

With white light a biquartz is used: two semicircular plates of right- and left-handed quartz, each 3.75 mm thick, which turn the plane of yellow light by 90° in opposite directions. When yellow is cut off from both halves they show the same grayish-violet tint of passage; a small turn makes one half reddish and the other bluish.

Half-shade device

Procedure

  1. Weigh 8 g of cane sugar, dissolve it in distilled water and make it up to 100 ml; filter it. This is the 8 % (0.08 g/cc) solution. Prepare 4 %, 2 %, 1 % and 0.5 % solutions by successive dilution (50 ml of solution + 50 ml of water).
  2. Find the least count of the vernier attached to the analyser.
  3. Clean the windows of the polarimeter tube and fill it with distilled water so that no air bubble is enclosed after the end caps are tightened.
  4. Place the tube horizontally in the polarimeter; if a small bubble remains, bring it into the bulged portion in the middle of the tube.
  5. Switch on the lamp, look through the eyepiece and focus it sharply on the line dividing the two halves of the half-shade device.
  6. Rotate the analyser slowly clockwise until the two halves appear exactly identical (equally dim; with a biquartz, the same grayish-violet tint). Note the reading — first position a0.
  7. Rotate the analyser (through about 180°) until the two halves are once again identical and note the second position b0.
  8. Empty the tube, rinse it with the 8 % sugar solution, fill it with this solution without air bubbles, tighten the caps and place it in the polarimeter.
  9. Take the readings a1 and b1 as in steps 6 and 7. Repeat for the 4 %, 2 %, 1 % and 0.5 % solutions.
  10. Find the rotation for each solution by subtracting the water readings: θ1 = a1 − a0, θ2 = b1 − b0, and take the mean θ.
  11. Measure the length l of the tube in decimetre and calculate S = θ/(l C) for each solution.
  12. Plot a graph of θ against C; it is a straight line through the origin. Find θ/C from its slope and S = (θ/C)/l.

Precautions

  1. Rinse the polarimeter tube with the corresponding solution (or water) before each observation.
  2. Screw the end caps properly after filling the solution or water — not so tight that the glass windows are strained.
  3. Keep the end windows clean; do not touch them with the fingers.
  4. The path of light through the solution must be free from air bubbles.
  5. Filter the solution before use.
  6. Set the position of equal illumination (or the sensitive tint) precisely each time.
  7. Rotate the analyser in one direction only while taking one set of readings (do not turn it to and fro, to avoid backlash).
